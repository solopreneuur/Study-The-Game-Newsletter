# Study the Game — #00
_The 5 best ideas I hand-picked this week_

**1. A Belgian grocer renamed carrots "orange rockets" and sales jumped 151%** ★ my pick
Delhaize relabeled produce for kids with playful names. Carrots became "Orange Rockets," courgettes became "Troll Bats," with no change to the product or the price. Vegetable sales to families rose 151%. The only thing that moved was the word on the sign.
**How I'm stealing it:** Before I change a product to make it more appealing, I'll try changing what I call it first.

**2. Bricked-up windows across Britain are scars from a 155-year-old tax**
Starting in 1696, Britain taxed houses by their window count, as a visible proxy for wealth. Owners bricked up windows to dip below the taxable threshold, and you can still see them on old buildings today. The tax lasted until 1851 and is blamed for a rise in damp- and dark-related illness: a measurement reshaping the thing it was only supposed to observe.
**How I'm stealing it:** Whenever I set a metric as a target, I'll ask what people would physically build or hide to game it.

**3. Xerox's biggest decision was refusing to sell its own copier**
The 1959 Xerox 914 cost $29,500, too much for most offices to buy outright. So Xerox leased it for a small monthly fee plus a few cents per copy. By 1965 the 914 alone brought in roughly two-thirds of Xerox's revenue. The CEO called the leasing decision more important than the technology itself.
**How I'm stealing it:** When a price is the real obstacle, I'll look for a way to charge for usage instead of ownership before I touch the product.

**4. A server who gave candy twice beat one who gave it once**
Researchers tested giving restaurant diners a mint with the check. One piece raised tips a little. Two pieces at once pushed them up about 14%. The biggest jump came when the server dropped off one piece, walked away, then came back with a second. Same two candies, but now it read as spontaneous generosity instead of policy.
**How I'm stealing it:** I'm adding a small, unscripted second touch to things I already do for people, instead of bundling the whole gesture up front.

**5. Billionaire investor Sam Zell banned deal memos longer than one page**
No matter how complicated the real estate deal, Sam Zell required the written brief to fit on a single page. Compressing a deal to one page forces whoever wrote it to understand which details matter and which are noise, before anyone commits money.
**How I'm stealing it:** Before I approve or pitch anything complicated, I'll write the one-page version first.
