**1. A server who gave candy twice beat one who gave it once** · _psychology_
Researchers tested giving restaurant diners a mint with the check. A single piece raised tips modestly. Giving two pieces at once pushed the average up about 14%. But the biggest jump came when a server dropped off one piece, walked off, then came back and added a second — same two pieces of candy, but it now read as spontaneous generosity instead of policy.
**How I'm stealing it:** I'm adding a small, unscripted second touch to things I already do for people, instead of bundling the whole gesture up front.
Source: https://ecommons.cornell.edu/entities/publication/49ee9efd-b182-49af-8346-a3c63391a552

**2. The cherry-tree story that isn't true still makes kids more honest** · _psychology_
Psychologists read kids one of four stories, then tempted them to cheat in a game. Pinocchio and The Boy Who Cried Wolf — both built around punishment for lying — did nothing to reduce lying. Only the fabricated tale of young George Washington confessing to cutting down his father's cherry tree, and being praised for the truth, significantly increased honesty. Reward the confession, not the fear, and the story works.
**How I'm stealing it:** When I want someone to come clean about a mistake, I'll make the story end in praise for admitting it, not punishment for the act.
Source: https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/george-washington-trumps-pinocchio-for-inspiring-honesty-in-kids-study-says.html

**3. 69% of innocent people signed a confession for something they didn't do** · _psychology_
In a 1996 lab study, participants were warned that hitting the "Alt" key would crash a computer and destroy data. None of them hit it — but when the computer was secretly made to crash anyway, 69% signed a written confession admitting they did, a quarter actually came to believe it, and some invented specific details of how.
**How I'm stealing it:** I'm treating any "confession" obtained under pressure or accusation as unreliable evidence, including my own.
Source: https://sites.psu.edu/aspsy/2023/03/01/false-confessions-2/

**4. The same wine tastes better to your brain once it costs more** · _psychology_
Researchers gave people wine samples labeled with different prices, scanning their brains while they drank. Unknown to the drinkers, some of the "different" wines were identical liquid under a new price tag. The $90 label produced more activity in the brain's pleasure center than the $10 label on the exact same wine — price was shaping the actual experience, not just the opinion of it.
**How I'm stealing it:** Before I trust my own enthusiasm for something expensive, I'll ask what I'd think of it blind.
Source: https://www.caltech.edu/about/news/wine-study-shows-price-influences-perception-1374

**5. 21 of 22 nurses were ready to double a patient's drug dose on a stranger's phone call** · _psychology_
A researcher posing as "Dr. Smith" phoned nurses and ordered an unlisted drug at double the labeled maximum dose — a call that broke hospital policy in three separate ways. A hidden observer stopped the nurses before any drug was given, but 21 of 22 were already complying. Asked beforehand what they'd do in that situation, almost none said they would.
**How I'm stealing it:** When a request arrives with borrowed authority and no time to check it, I'll treat the urgency itself as the red flag.
Source: https://www.simplypsychology.org/hofling-obedience.html

Delhaize relabeled produce for kids with playful names — carrots became "Orange Rockets," courgettes became "Troll Bats" — with no change to the product or the price. Vegetable sales to families rose 151%. The only thing that moved was the word on the sign.
**How I'm stealing it:** Before I change a product to make it more appealing, I'll try changing what I call it first.

**7. A jewelry company invented "a month's salary" out of nothing, and it became a law** · _marketing_
In 1947, De Beers' ad agency coined "A Diamond is Forever" and told men to spend a month's salary (later two) on an engagement ring — a number with no basis beyond being memorable. U.S. bride diamond ownership rose from 10% in 1940 to 80% by 1980; in Japan, where diamond rings barely existed, it went from under 5% in 1960 to 60% by 1981, after the same campaign was exported.
**How I'm stealing it:** When I want a new behavior to spread, I'll give it a specific, arbitrary-sounding rule of thumb rather than a vague suggestion.

**8. Removing the dollar sign from a menu made diners spend 8% more** · _marketing_
Cornell researchers gave diners at a real restaurant one of three menus: "$12," "12," or "twelve dollars." People who saw no dollar sign at all spent about 8% more per person than those who saw the symbol — the spelled-out version performed no better than the symbol. Removing the visual reminder of money reduced what researchers call the "pain of paying."
**How I'm stealing it:** On anything I'm pricing myself, I'll test dropping the currency symbol before I touch the number.
Source: https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2009/12/beware-menus-dont-use-dollar-signs

**9. A big, real-money study found the famous "$9.99 feels cheaper" effect might not exist** · _marketing_
266 people made 4,788 real purchase decisions with their own money, testing whether prices ending in 9 (like $1.99) genuinely feel cheaper than round prices. The result: no statistically significant effect either way. A piece of pricing lore repeated in marketing classes for decades turned out to be far shakier than assumed once someone ran the numbers properly.
**How I'm stealing it:** I'm going to stop assuming a "well-known" tactic works just because everyone cites it, and check if anyone's actually tested it on money people spend.
Source: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0270850

**10. Ogilvy's most famous headline was lifted almost word for word from a magazine** · _marketing_
David Ogilvy's legendary 1958 Rolls-Royce line — "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise comes from the electric clock" — came, by his own account, from a technical editor's write-up in a British car magazine. A near-identical line had already run in a 1933 Pierce-Arrow ad. Ogilvy called it the best headline he ever wrote. Rolls-Royce sales rose 50% that year anyway.
**How I'm stealing it:** When I'm stuck for the right line, I'll go read what the technical experts already wrote about the thing, instead of trying to invent language from scratch.

**11. Xerox's biggest decision was refusing to sell its own copier** · _business_
The 1959 Xerox 914 cost $29,500 — too much for most offices to buy outright. So Xerox leased it instead, for a small monthly fee plus a few cents per copy, letting customers who'd never pay the sticker price use it anyway. The 914 alone generated roughly two-thirds of Xerox's revenue by 1965. The CEO called the leasing decision more important than the technology itself.
**How I'm stealing it:** When a price is the real obstacle, I'll look for a way to charge for usage instead of ownership before I touch the product.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerox_914

**12. Southwest Airlines' famous 10-minute turnaround started because it had to sell a plane** · _business_
In 1972, broke and down to $143 in the bank, Southwest sold one of its four aircraft to raise cash — then had to keep flying the same schedule with one less plane. VP Bill Franklin's fix was to unload, clean, refuel, and reload each aircraft in 10 minutes instead of the usual 25. The airline posted its first profit the following year and has never lost money since.
**How I'm stealing it:** Next time I'm short a resource, I'll look for the process fix before I look for more budget.
Source: https://www.tpr.org/business/2015-06-28/the-man-who-saved-southwest-airlines-with-a-10-minute-idea

**13. Zappos pays brand-new employees to quit, on purpose** · _business_
After a multi-week training period, Zappos offers every new hire a few thousand dollars, no questions asked, to walk away instead of taking the job. Only 2–3% take the money. The logic: it's far cheaper to pay off a bad cultural fit in week one than to discover it a year in, and the offer itself filters for people who actually want to be there.
**How I'm stealing it:** I'll build in an explicit, easy exit ramp early in any commitment, so the people who stay are staying on purpose.
Source: https://www.redstrategygroup.com/insights/2016/2/26/why-zappos-offers-new-hires-2000-to-quit

**14. The credit card's founding myth was invented by its own PR man** · _business_
The standard Diners Club origin story has Frank McNamara forgetting his wallet at a 1949 dinner and inventing the charge card on the spot. Diners Club's own first press agent, Matty Simmons, later admitted flatly that it never happened — he made it up because it was a better story than the real, slower founding. The fake version is still the one everyone tells.
**How I'm stealing it:** When I'm explaining why something I built exists, I'll check whether I'm repeating the convenient version of the story or the true one.

**15. Rolex could make more watches. It chooses the waiting list instead** · _business_
Rolex produces roughly a million watches a year — far short of demand, with waits of up to a decade for some models — despite having the capacity and capital to scale up significantly. Industry analysts describe the scarcity as a deliberate lever, not a limitation: it protects full retail pricing, props up resale value, and turns a purchase into an achievement instead of a transaction.
**How I'm stealing it:** Before I treat a shortage as a problem to fix, I'll ask whether the shortage is actually doing useful work.

**21. Bricked-up windows across Britain are scars from a 155-year-old tax** · _models_
Starting in 1696, Britain taxed houses by their window count, using it as a visible proxy for wealth. Owners responded by permanently bricking up windows to dip below the taxable threshold — a workaround still visible on old buildings today. The tax lasted until 1851 and is blamed for a rise in damp- and dark-related illness: a clean case of a measurement reshaping the thing it was supposed to just observe.
**How I'm stealing it:** Whenever I set a metric as a target, I'll ask what people would physically build or hide to game it.

**24. A one-page checklist took a hospital's infection rate to zero** · _models_
Johns Hopkins doctor Peter Pronovost wrote a five-step checklist for inserting central IV lines — things like washing hands and using full sterile barriers — assuming these infections were simply inevitable. Michigan rolled it out across more than 100 ICUs; within 18 months, most reported zero central-line infections, sustained for years, with an estimated 1,500 lives and $100 million saved. The fix wasn't new medicine. It was making sure the known steps actually happened every time.
**How I'm stealing it:** For my own recurring high-stakes task, I'll write the boring five-step checklist instead of trusting memory and experience.

**25. "Because I have to make copies" worked almost as well as a real reason** · _models_
In a classic experiment, a researcher tried to cut in line at a copy machine using no reason, a real reason, or a circular non-reason — "because I have to make copies." For a small request, the fake reason boosted compliance from 60% to 93%, nearly matching the real one. For a bigger request, the reason stopped mattering at all: compliance sat at 24% whether one was given or not. The word "because" does the work, until the stakes are big enough that people actually listen to what follows it.
**How I'm stealing it:** For small asks, I'll always attach a reason, any reason — and save my best real reasons for the requests that are big enough to need them.
Source: https://siliconcanals.com/t-copier-experiment-because-small-big-favours/
