**1. Houston airport ended baggage complaints by making the walk six times longer** · _psychology_
Passengers kept complaining about bag waits, even after extra handlers cut the wait to about eight minutes. The airport found people walked one minute and then stood for seven. So it moved arrivals farther away and sent bags to the farthest carousel. The walk became six times longer, and complaints nearly disappeared. People don't hate waiting. They hate idle waiting.
**How I'm stealing it:** I'll give anyone waiting on me something to do or see, like a progress update or a first draft, instead of silence.
Source: https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/why-waiting-is-torture.html

**2. A longer colonoscopy was remembered as less painful** · _psychology_
In a trial with 682 patients, doctors added a short, milder stretch to the end of half the procedures. Those patients endured more discomfort in total, but they rated the final moments less painful (1.7 vs. 2.5 out of 10). They remembered the whole thing as better and were more likely to come back for a repeat screening. Memory keeps the peak and the ending, not the total.
**How I'm stealing it:** I'll end every call and client handoff on the easiest, best part, not the hardest one.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12855328/

**3. Spilling coffee made the expert more likable** · _psychology_
In 1966, Elliot Aronson had students listen to a quiz contestant who got 92% of the questions right. In one version, the contestant spilled coffee on himself at the end. Students liked the clumsy version more. The same blunder made an average contestant less likable. A slip only humanizes you once people already think you're competent.
**How I'm stealing it:** After I've shown I know my stuff, I'll admit one small mistake openly instead of hiding it.
Source: https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/why-admitting-a-weakness-makes-people-like-you-more.html

**4. "Because I have to make copies" worked almost as well as a real reason** · _psychology_
Ellen Langer had a researcher ask to cut in line at a copier. With no reason, 60% said yes. With "because I'm in a rush," 94% said yes. With the meaningless "because I have to make copies," 93% said yes. For small asks, people respond to the shape of a reason, and the word "because," more than to what the reason says.
**How I'm stealing it:** Every request I send this week will include a "because," even a short one.
Source: https://jamesclear.com/copy-machine-study

**5. Thank-you letters land far better than the writer expects** · _psychology_
Amit Kumar and Nicholas Epley had people write gratitude letters and predict how the recipient would feel. In four experiments, writers overestimated how awkward recipients would feel and underestimated how happy they'd be. Writers worried about saying it well. Recipients only noticed the warmth. That wrong forecast is why most thank-yous never get sent.
**How I'm stealing it:** I'll send one specific thank-you message on Monday to someone who changed my trajectory, without polishing it.
Source: https://news.uchicago.edu/story/people-underestimate-value-sending-letters-appreciation

**6. One more follow-up question per date means one more second date** · _psychology_
Harvard researchers analyzed more than 2,000 speed-dating conversations. People who asked more questions, especially follow-up questions, got more second dates. The researchers estimated that one extra question on each of 20 dates would win about one more yes. People don't predict this. They think talking about themselves is what makes them impressive.
**How I'm stealing it:** On my next sales or networking call, I'll ask at least three "what happened next?" questions before I pitch anything.
Source: https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/asking-questions-can-get-you-a-better-job-or-a-second-date

**7. A sign saying "many visitors steal wood" quadrupled theft** · _psychology_
At Petrified Forest National Park, Robert Cialdini's team tested signs. "Please don't remove the petrified wood" cut theft to under 2%. A sign saying that many past visitors had taken wood pushed theft to about 8%. Warning people that "everyone does it" tells them it's normal.
**How I'm stealing it:** I'll stop writing "most people forget to…" in my emails and point to what the people who succeed do instead.
Source: https://www.psychologytoday.com/za/blog/the-shaping-us/201909/the-petrified-wood-principle

**8. A free two-stamp head start nearly doubled loyalty-card completion** · _marketing_
A car wash gave 300 customers one of two cards. One needed 8 stamps. The other needed 10 but came with 2 already stamped. Both required eight washes. Over nine months, 19% finished the blank card and 34% finished the pre-stamped one. People stick with goals they believe they've already started.
**How I'm stealing it:** My onboarding will mark step one complete the moment someone signs up.
Source: https://coglode.com/nuggets/endowed-progress-effect

**9. The Michelin Guide exists to wear out your tires** · _marketing_
In 1900, France had fewer than 3,000 cars. The Michelin brothers, who sold tires, gave away about 35,000 guides listing mechanics, hotels, and petrol stations, so people would drive more and wear out more tires. The guide was free for two decades. They started charging after André saw copies propping up a workbench and decided people only respect what they pay for.
**How I'm stealing it:** I'll create one free resource that gets customers using the thing my product depends on.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelin_Guide

**10. The same wine tasted better, in the brain, at $90 than at $10** · _marketing_
Caltech researchers scanned 20 people tasting wines. The same wine was presented once at $90 and once at $10, and a $5 wine was relabeled $45. Higher prices raised both people's pleasure ratings and activity in a brain region that tracks pleasure. Without price tags, tasters rated the $5 wine best. Price isn't only a cost. It's part of the product.
**How I'm stealing it:** I'll test a higher price on one offer this week and watch whether perceived quality goes up with it.
Source: https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/93947.php

**11. Pencils are yellow to signal Chinese graphite** · _marketing_
At the 1889 Paris Exposition, the Hardtmuth company showed a pencil painted glossy yellow and named it Koh-i-Noor, after the famous diamond. The best graphite then came from China, where yellow signaled royalty and prestige. Yellow became shorthand for "premium inside," and competitors copied it until it was the default.
**How I'm stealing it:** I'll pick one visual cue that signals our best quality and use it everywhere, so people link it to us.
Source: https://www.mentalfloss.com/history/why-pencils-are-yellow

**12. People preferred the slower website that showed its work** · _marketing_
Harvard's Ryan Buell and Michael Norton tested travel and dating sites. People valued results more when the site showed the work happening, such as each airline it was checking, even when it took longer. Most chose the slower, transparent site over an instant one. Visible effort feels like effort spent on you.
**How I'm stealing it:** When I deliver work, I'll show the three options I rejected along with the one I picked.
Source: https://whyisthisinteresting.substack.com/p/why-is-this-interesting-the-loading

**13. Stripe's founders didn't send links. They took your laptop** · _business_
Most founders ask whether you'll try their beta and promise to send a link. Patrick and John Collison said, "Right then, give me your laptop," and set Stripe up on the spot. Y Combinator now calls this the "Collison installation." The gap between "yes, I'll try it" and actually trying it is where most early users drop off.
**How I'm stealing it:** When someone says "sounds interesting," I'll set them up during that same call, not afterward.
Source: https://paulgraham.com/ds.html

**14. "If you raise the effing hot dog, I will kill you"** · _business_
Costco's hot dog combo has cost $1.50 since 1984. When future CEO Craig Jelinek said it was losing money, co-founder Jim Sinegal replied, "If you raise the effing hot dog, I will kill you. Figure it out." So Costco built its own hot dog factories rather than raise the price. A fixed price constraint pushed them to rebuild the supply chain.
**How I'm stealing it:** I'll pick one promise I won't change, like a price or a turnaround time, and make the business work around it.
Source: https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/547020/costco-150-hot-dog-soda-combo-enigma

**15. Van Halen's "no brown M&Ms" rule was a safety test** · _business_
Van Halen's touring contract buried a line deep in its technical rider: a bowl of M&Ms, no brown ones. If David Lee Roth saw a brown M&M backstage, he knew the promoter hadn't read the contract closely and had the whole production line-checked. At one Colorado show, the stage sank into a new floor and caused heavy damage. The candy was a cheap way to check that the important parts had been read.
**How I'm stealing it:** I'll add one small, specific instruction to my next brief to see whether it was read closely.
Source: https://ultimateclassicrock.com/david-lee-roth-van-halen-brown-mms-rule/

**16. IKEA's flat-pack started with a table that wouldn't fit in a car** · _business_
In 1956, IKEA designer Gillis Lundgren couldn't fit a three-legged leaf table into a car after a catalogue shoot. According to company lore, he took the legs off. That workaround became flat-pack furniture: cheaper shipping, smaller stores, and customers doing the assembly. A packing problem became the business model.
**How I'm stealing it:** I'll list the most annoying part of delivering my product and ask what would happen if I made it the customer's job, at a lower price.
Source: https://www.dezeen.com/2013/07/22/ikea-revives-three-legged-diy-side-table/

**17. Nintendo won handhelds with deliberately old technology** · _business_
Gunpei Yokoi's rule was "lateral thinking with withered technology": use cheap, mature parts in new ways. For the Game Boy, he passed on a color screen. Rivals like Sega's Game Gear and Atari's Lynx went with color and drained batteries. The black-and-white Game Boy ran far longer and beat them both.
**How I'm stealing it:** On my next build, I'll choose the boring, proven tool and spend the difference on the experience.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunpei_Yokoi

**18. Bell Labs' best researchers kept their doors open** · _people_
Richard Hamming noticed that colleagues who closed their office doors got more done today and tomorrow. Over the years, the open-door people did the more important work. The interruptions gave them clues about what problems actually mattered. Focus wins in the short term. Exposure decides what you focus on.
**How I'm stealing it:** I'll protect mornings for deep work and keep one afternoon a week completely open for unplanned conversations.
Source: https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~robins/YouAndYourResearch.html

**19. Hemingway always quit while he knew what came next** · _people_
In a 1935 Esquire piece, Hemingway gave his rule: "always stop when you are going good and when you know what will happen next." Stopping mid-flow means the next session starts with momentum instead of a blank page. He said a writer who did this every day would never get stuck.
**How I'm stealing it:** I'll end each work session by writing the first line of tomorrow's task, then stop.
Source: https://fs.blog/ernest-hemingway-three-tips-on-how-to-write-fiction/

**20. Darwin wrote down every fact that contradicted him, immediately** · _people_
Darwin followed what he called a "golden rule." Whenever he came across a fact or thought that went against his conclusions, he made a note of it right away. He had found that inconvenient evidence slipped from memory far faster than evidence that agreed with him. As a result, he said, few objections were ever raised that he hadn't already considered.
**How I'm stealing it:** I'll start a running "evidence I'm wrong" note for my current biggest bet.
Source: https://stafforini.com/quotes/darwin-1958-autobiography-charles-darwin-q-1163790d/

**21. Seinfeld says "Don't break the chain" was never his idea** · _people_
"Don't break the chain" is one of the most repeated productivity tips: mark an X on the calendar every day you write. It's credited to Jerry Seinfeld. In a 2014 Reddit AMA, Seinfeld said it wasn't his idea, and he never claimed to use it. A famous name helped the tip spread more than evidence that it works.
**How I'm stealing it:** Before I share advice credited to someone famous, I'll find where they actually said it.
Source: https://jamesclear.com/stop-procrastinating-seinfeld-strategy

**22. FedEx fixed its night shift by changing one word: hour to shift** · _models_
Charlie Munger's favorite incentive story: FedEx couldn't get packages through its central hub fast enough each night. Pep talks and pressure failed. Then someone noticed workers were paid by the hour, which rewarded going slow. FedEx switched to paying by the shift, letting people go home when the work was done, and the problem disappeared.
**How I'm stealing it:** I'll find one way I pay for time instead of results, with myself or a contractor, and flip it.
Source: https://jamesclear.com/great-speeches/psychology-of-human-misjudgment-by-charlie-munger

**23. Imagine it already failed, then explain why** · _models_
Psychologist Gary Klein's "premortem" asks a team to assume the project has already failed and write down why. That framing makes it safe to voice doubts people would otherwise keep to themselves. Klein cites 1989 research finding that imagining an outcome has already happened improves people's ability to identify why it would happen by about 30%.
**How I'm stealing it:** Before my next launch, I'll spend 10 minutes writing "it flopped because…" and fix the top two reasons.
Source: https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem

**24. Kahneman made army interviewers score traits before trusting their gut** · _models_
As a young officer in the Israeli army, Daniel Kahneman found that recruit interviews barely predicted anything. He replaced them with six traits, each scored separately from factual questions. Only at the end could interviewers "close their eyes" and give a gut score. The structured scores predicted far better, and the gut score only became useful once it came last.
**How I'm stealing it:** For my next hire, I'll write down three traits and score each one before deciding how I feel overall.
Source: https://edbatista.com/2021/02/daniel-kahneman-on-conducting-better-interviews.html

**25. Hanoi paid a bounty for rat tails and got rat farms** · _models_
In 1902, French officials in Hanoi paid one cent per rat and asked only for the tail as proof. Soon tailless rats were running through the city. Hunters were cutting off tails and releasing the rats to breed. Inspectors later found farms raising rats just for their tails. The bounty made the rat problem worse, and it was cancelled.
**How I'm stealing it:** For every metric I reward, I'll ask how someone could hit the number while making the real problem worse.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Hanoi_Rat_Massacre
